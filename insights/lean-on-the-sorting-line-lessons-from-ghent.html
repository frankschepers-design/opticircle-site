Lean on the sorting line: lessons from building and running a modern sorting plant

Frank Schepers · 1 November 2026

Between 2018 and 2021 I designed, built, started up and then ran PreZero's sorting plant in Ghent, Belgium. Before that I had spent fifteen years at SUEZ and SITA, the last eleven as Director Asset Management for the Benelux and Germany. In 2019, in the middle of the Ghent project, I completed a Lean Black Belt at Cardiff University.

Doing those two things at the same time taught me more than either would have alone. This note is not about one plant's numbers. It is about the practices I would bring to any sorting operation, from a large European facility to a new line in Ulaanbaatar.

1. The input decides almost everything

Lean starts with understanding what the customer values. In a sorting plant there are two customers: the one who delivers the waste and the one who buys the bales. Between them sits the input, and its composition varies by the hour. The best investment I know is a disciplined routine of sampling and recording what actually arrives, by supplier and by season. Without it, every adjustment to the line is a guess.

2. Design for maintenance, not for the brochure

Sorting lines are sold on throughput and purity. They are run on access and uptime. During design, ask the maintenance team, not only the supplier, how they will reach every screen, belt and optical sorter, how long a cleaning stop takes, and where the spares will sit. A plant that is hard to clean will be cleaned less often, and its performance will drift quietly.

3. Start-up is a project of its own

Commissioning is not the last week of construction; it is the first months of operation, and it deserves its own plan, budget and leader. Ramp up in steps, with each stage held until it is stable. The temptation to push full volume through a line that is not yet balanced is strong, especially with waste piling up in the hall. Resist it. Problems hidden at start-up become habits.

4. Go and see

The Lean principle of gemba, going to where the work happens, matters more in sorting than almost anywhere. Data from the control room tells you that something has changed. Only the floor tells you why: a wrapped shaft, a blocked chute, a bale that looks wrong. I made a habit of walking the line at the start of each shift, and I would ask any plant manager to do the same.

5. Make the line visible

A sorting line produces a lot of information and little of it is seen by the people who could act on it. Simple visual management works: a board at the line showing the day's plan, the stops and their causes, and the quality of the last bales. Keep the measures few and in the operators' own words. A shift team that can see its own performance will start to improve it without being asked.

6. Standard work, especially for stops and changeovers

Most lost time on a sorting line comes from the same handful of events: blockages, cleaning, changes of input. Each deserves a written, practised, standard way of being handled, with the safety steps built in. Standard work is not bureaucracy. It is what lets a new operator perform like an experienced one and lets you see clearly when something is genuinely abnormal.

7. Fix root causes, not symptoms

When a belt blocks for the third time in a week, clearing it is the symptom. Asking "why" until you reach the cause, whether that is input quality, a transfer point or a setting, is the work. Simple tools such as the five whys and a Pareto of stop causes are enough to start. The discipline is in doing it every week.

8. People first

Sorting is physical, repetitive and, if it is not managed well, dangerous work. The plants that perform best are the ones where operators are trained properly, listened to, and trusted to stop the line when something is wrong. Every Lean improvement I have seen last was started by someone who works on the floor.

What this means for new markets

In Mongolia and across much of Asia, new recycling and sorting capacity is being planned. The technology is available from many suppliers. What makes the difference is operating discipline, and that can be built in from the first drawing rather than added after the first crisis.
