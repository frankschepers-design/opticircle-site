Your MSE ESG waste evidence pack: the documents I ask for first

Frank Schepers · 6 November 2026

In my earlier note I set out why waste is the place to start for the Mongolian Stock Exchange ESG template, and a month-by-month plan to 30 April 2027.1 This companion is more practical: the short list of operational documents I ask for first when I sit with a Tier I issuer, how I would organise them, and the gaps that show up again and again.

None of this is exotic. If your sites already move waste, you almost certainly hold most of these papers. The work is to gather them, label them, and turn them into a line the board can stand behind in English.

What I ask for in the first week

I start with six document types. Together they cover what left each site, who took it, and where it went.

  • Site and contractor map. A simple list of every operating site (plant, warehouse, office, project) and every waste contractor or haulier used in the reporting year. Names, contract start dates, and which streams each contractor takes.
  • Weighbridge tickets and transfer notes. The primary evidence of tonnes. Prefer originals or clear scans, by site and by month. If your site has no weighbridge, use the contractor's dock ticket or transfer note instead, and say so.
  • Contractor invoices for waste services. These reconcile cost and volume. I use them to catch missing loads and to separate disposal from recycling charges.
  • Hazardous-waste paperwork. Manifests, permits, and any authorised-disposal certificates your contractors provide. Hazardous and non-hazardous must be separable in the final table.
  • Recycling and recovery confirmations. Where material went to recycling, reuse or energy recovery, keep the confirmation that names the facility and the stream. A line on an invoice that only says "waste removal" is not enough.
  • Internal production or stock records for the same period. Not for the waste line itself, but to sense-check that the tonnes look plausible against what the plant made or shipped.

I do not ask for a finished ESG narrative in week one. I ask for the evidence that will make the narrative honest.

How I organise the pack

A folder structure beats a beautiful spreadsheet that nobody can audit. I use one folder per site, and inside it one folder per month for tickets and invoices. At the top of each site folder I keep a one-page index:

  1. Site name and address
  2. Contractors and streams
  3. What is measured (weighbridge, contractor ticket, estimate)
  4. What is estimated, and the method
  5. Any gaps for the year (missing months, missing streams)

Then one working workbook that rolls every site into hazardous / non-hazardous and into destination (recycling, landfill, other). The workbook is the draft of the template line; the folders are what an external reviewer, a bank or a board member can open if they ask "how do you know?"

File searchable PDFs where you can. An independent study of first-round MSE ESG filings found that about half of the documents on the public registry were scanned images with no usable text.2 Do not add your evidence pack to that problem.

Common gaps I see

  • One contractor invoice for the whole company. It hides site differences and makes year-on-year comparison impossible. Split by site, even if the invoice is central.
  • Recycling claimed without a destination. The template and a careful reader will ask where it went. If you cannot name the facility, treat the tonnes as "other" until you can.
  • Hazardous mixed into general waste. Or the opposite: every oily rag listed as hazardous with no permit trail. Separate the streams and keep the paperwork that matches the classification.
  • Missing months filled with a round number. If you must estimate, write the method on the site index and keep it consistent. The disclosure rules expect you to explain gaps and the steps you are taking to close them.3
  • English left to the last week. Tier I issuers are expected to publish the information in English.1,4 Translate the waste table and the gap notes early; leave the prose polish for later.

What "good enough for April" looks like

By the time you draft the template in February, I want to see twelve months of records by stream and destination, a clear note of what was measured versus estimated, and a short English explanation for every cell you cannot yet fill. That is enough for a credible waste line. Energy, water and workforce data can use the same site map and the same calendar.
